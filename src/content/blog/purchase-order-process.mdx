If you've read the [PO lifecycle from request to payment](/blog/purchase-order-process-explained), you know the eight stages. This post zooms in on the workflow side: **who does what at each step, where things break, and how lightweight tooling fits without paying for a procurement suite**.

This is for the operations person who's trying to clean up an ad-hoc purchasing process — not the enterprise buyer comparing Coupa to Ariba.

## The five roles

Most PO workflows touch five distinct roles. In small businesses, one person plays several of them. In larger ones, each is a separate seat.

**Requester** — the person who wants to buy something. Files the purchase request, justifies the need, identifies the vendor.

**Approver** — the person with budget authority. Approves or rejects the request based on budget, business case, and policy compliance.

**Buyer** — the person who issues the PO to the vendor. Often the same as the requester in small operations; a dedicated procurement role in larger ones.

**Vendor** — the seller. Receives the PO, accepts it, fulfills the order, sends the invoice.

**AP (accounts payable)** — the team that processes the invoice, matches it against the PO and receipt, and pays the vendor.

Knowing which role you're playing — and which role you're handing off to next — is half the battle. Most PO mistakes happen at handoffs.

## The six-step workflow

### Step 1: Identify the need

**Who:** Requester
**Output:** Purchase request (sometimes called requisition)

Someone in the organization realizes they need something. The need might be obvious (the printer is broken) or planned (Q3 software renewals). Either way, the first step is documenting *what* and *why*.

In small businesses, this is often a Slack message: "Hey, we need to renew the Notion subscription, can I get approval?" In larger ones, it's a formal purchase requisition with item numbers, quantities, vendor recommendations, and a business case.

**Common breakdown:** Requests get raised verbally or in chat with no paper trail. Approver loses track. Two weeks later, requester complains nothing happened. Solution: even a one-line written request is better than a verbal one.

**Tooling fit:** A shared Google Form or Notion database for requests is enough for most teams. ERP-grade requisition systems are overkill below ~50 employees.

### Step 2: Approve the request

**Who:** Approver
**Output:** Approved request, ready to convert to a PO

The approver checks budget, business need, and policy compliance. For small purchases, this might be a quick "yes" in chat. For larger ones, it might require multiple sign-offs.

**Approval thresholds** are the lever here. Common patterns:

- Under $500: requester self-approves
- $500–$5,000: department head approves
- $5,000–$25,000: department head + finance
- $25,000+: executive approval required

These thresholds don't have to be rigid — they just need to exist so people know who to ask.

**Common breakdown:** Approver is on vacation. No backup. Requests pile up. Vendor relationship damaged because nothing happens for two weeks.

**Tooling fit:** Slack workflows, an approval column in a shared sheet, or a lightweight tool like Asana with custom fields all work. The key feature is **a backup approver baked in** — never single-thread approvals on one person.

### Step 3: Issue the PO

**Who:** Buyer
**Output:** Purchase order sent to vendor

Once approved, someone has to convert the request into an actual PO and send it to the vendor. This is the moment the request becomes a binding commitment.

The PO should include:
- PO number (sequential, unique)
- Vendor and buyer contact info
- Itemized list with quantities and prices
- Total amount
- Payment terms (Net 30, Net 60, etc.)
- Delivery date and address
- Reference to any quote or contract

**Common breakdown:** Buyer reuses a Word template, forgets to update half the fields. PO goes out with last buyer's name in the contact field. Vendor calls the wrong person; delivery scheduled to the wrong address.

**Tooling fit:** This is exactly the step where a free [PO generator](/purchase-order-generator) earns its keep. You enter the new info, the rest is templated, and the PDF is ready in 90 seconds. Templates in [Excel or Word](/purchase-order-template) work fine for low volume.

### Step 4: Vendor acceptance and fulfillment

**Who:** Vendor
**Output:** Goods or services delivered, invoice sent

The vendor confirms the PO (sometimes with a [sales order](/blog/purchase-order-vs-invoice-vs-sales-order) sent back as acknowledgment), schedules fulfillment, ships the goods or delivers the services, and sends an invoice.

This step is mostly out of your hands, but two things are worth tracking:

- **Did the vendor confirm receipt of the PO?** Silence isn't acceptance. A confirmation email or signed PO acknowledgment is the standard.
- **Did the vendor's acknowledgment match what you sent?** Vendors sometimes "confirm" with subtle changes (different lead times, surcharges, modified terms). Read the acknowledgment carefully.

**Common breakdown:** Vendor invoices for slightly different terms than the PO (different price, added shipping, expedited fee). Buyer doesn't notice. AP pays the higher amount because the invoice "matches the goods received."

**Tooling fit:** A simple "PO sent / acknowledged / fulfilled" status column in your tracking sheet covers this. The point is having visibility — not building a CRM.

### Step 5: Receipt and matching

**Who:** Receiver (often the requester) → AP
**Output:** Receipt confirmation matched to PO

When the goods arrive (or services are delivered), someone has to confirm what was received matches what was ordered. This is the **receiving** step.

Then AP performs the **three-way match**:

1. Does the **invoice** match the **PO**?
2. Does the **invoice** match the **receipt**?
3. Does the **receipt** match the **PO**?

If all three line up, AP processes payment. If any of them disagree, the discrepancy gets investigated.

**Common breakdown:** Receiving step gets skipped entirely. Invoice arrives. AP pays it without confirming the goods actually arrived. Vendor's "shipped" status meant "left our warehouse," not "arrived at yours."

**Tooling fit:** Even a simple "received / not received" checkbox in your PO tracking sheet, plus a habit of waiting for that checkbox before AP pays, prevents most receiving fraud and short-shipment issues.

### Step 6: Payment and close

**Who:** AP
**Output:** Vendor paid, PO closed

After successful matching, AP schedules payment per the agreed terms. Once payment clears, the PO is marked closed.

**Common breakdown:** POs sit "open" indefinitely because nobody bothers to mark them closed after payment. A year later, the open PO list is meaningless because half of them are actually done. Solution: include a "close PO" step in the AP workflow as standard practice.

**Tooling fit:** Most accounting systems (QuickBooks, Xero, Wave) handle the AP side. The PO tracking should sync with — or at least mirror — what's in accounting.

## Where the process usually breaks

In small-to-mid businesses, the process breaks in predictable places:

**Approval bottlenecks.** One person is the only approver; they go on vacation; nothing moves. Fix: name a backup for every approval threshold.

**Ad-hoc requests.** Requests come in via chat with no documentation; they get lost. Fix: a shared form (Google Forms, Notion, Tally) with required fields.

**Skipped receiving.** Goods arrive; nobody confirms; AP pays the invoice without verifying. Fix: receiving checkbox in PO tracker, AP only pays after it's checked.

**Stale open POs.** POs accumulate "open" status; nobody knows what's actually outstanding. Fix: monthly review of open POs; close completed ones.

**Vendor invoice mismatches.** Vendor invoices for amounts that don't match the PO; AP pays anyway. Fix: enforce three-way matching as a non-negotiable AP step.

## Tooling without an enterprise procurement suite

Coupa and Ariba are great if you're a Fortune 500. For everyone else, a lightweight stack works:

- **Requests**: Google Form or Notion database
- **Approvals**: Slack workflow or shared sheet column
- **PO creation**: Free [PO generator](/purchase-order-generator) or [PO templates](/purchase-order-template)
- **PO tracking**: Shared spreadsheet (Google Sheets, Airtable) with status columns
- **Receiving**: Same spreadsheet, receiving column
- **AP**: QuickBooks, Xero, or your existing accounting system

This stack costs ~$0 to ~$50/month depending on which tools you already have. It scales to hundreds of POs per month before you outgrow it.

The single biggest upgrade for most teams isn't software — it's **writing down the workflow** and **assigning backup roles** at each step.

## How a PO tool fits each step

For Steps 1–2 (request and approval), generic tools (forms, sheets, chat) usually win because they integrate with how the rest of the team already communicates.

For Step 3 (issue PO), a dedicated PO generator earns its keep. The output is a clean PDF with a PO number, formatted consistently, ready to send. This is where time savings compound.

For Steps 4–6 (vendor side, receiving, AP), the PO itself is the artifact that ties everything together. As long as your PO has a clear number and itemized totals, AP can match against it regardless of what tool generated it.

The takeaway: don't try to find one tool for the whole process. Pick the right tool for each step.

## Roles and tools by company size

**Solo / under 5 people**:
- All roles played by 1–2 people
- Tooling: PO generator + accounting software, that's it
- Approvals: informal, in your head

**5–20 people**:
- Roles start to separate (founder approves; ops issues POs; bookkeeper handles AP)
- Tooling: PO generator + shared sheet for tracking + accounting software
- Approvals: written, not chat-based

**20–100 people**:
- Dedicated finance person; multiple approvers per threshold
- Tooling: lightweight procurement add-on or robust spreadsheet workflow
- Approvals: documented thresholds; backups assigned

**100+ people**:
- Procurement function exists as its own role/team
- Tooling: dedicated procurement platform (Coupa, Ariba, Procurify, Tipalti)
- Approvals: enforced by software, not policy alone

## The bottom line

The PO process isn't complicated. It's six steps, five roles, and a handful of handoffs. What makes it break isn't volume — it's lack of clarity about *who does what* and *what artifacts get produced* at each step.

If you're cleaning up an ad-hoc PO process, start by writing down:

1. Who can request a purchase
2. Who approves at each spending threshold
3. Who issues the PO once approved
4. Who confirms goods received
5. Who handles AP

Then add the simplest possible tool for each step. Don't over-engineer. The [free PO generator](/purchase-order-generator) plus a shared sheet plus your existing accounting software covers 95% of small business needs.

The other 5% — the cases that genuinely need an enterprise procurement platform — usually announce themselves clearly. Until then, lightweight wins.
